FE Civil Transportation in Six Formulas: Curves, Sight Distance, Flow and Pavement

Transportation is one of the larger blocks on the FE Civil exam. The NCEES specification puts between 9 and 14 of the 110 scored items in this area, and the AceFEExam transportation engineering overview lists the five sub-topics the outline names: geometric design, pavement design, traffic capacity and flow, traffic control devices, and planning. The good news is that most of these questions come down to a short list of relationships that are printed in the FE Reference Handbook. The work is knowing which one applies and keeping the units straight.

Below are six of those relationships, each with a small example worked from scratch. The numbers are made up for practice; the formulas are the standard US customary forms.

1. Horizontal curve geometry

For a simple circular curve with radius R and deflection angle I:

  • Tangent length: T = R tan(I/2)
  • Curve length: L = πRI/180, with I in degrees

Example: R = 1,500 ft and I = 30°. Then T = 1,500 × tan 15° = 1,500 × 0.2679 = 401.9 ft, and L = π × 1,500 × 30 / 180 = 785.4 ft.

If the PI sits at station 25+00, the PC is 2,500 − 401.9 = 2,098.1 ft, or station 20+98.1. The PT is found by adding the curve length to the PC, not the tangent to the PI: 2,098.1 + 785.4 = 2,883.5 ft, station 28+83.5. Adding T to the PI would give 29+01.9, which is a classic wrong answer choice. The horizontal and vertical curves study unit covers the stationing convention in more detail.

2. Minimum radius from superelevation and friction

The point-mass relationship is e + f = V² / (15R), with V in mph and R in ft. Rearranged: R = V² / (15(e + f)).

Example: design speed 60 mph, maximum superelevation 0.08, side friction factor 0.12. R = 3,600 / (15 × 0.20) = 1,200 ft. A smaller radius would need more superelevation or more friction than the design allows.

3. Stopping sight distance

SSD is the distance travelled during perception-reaction time plus the braking distance:

SSD = 1.47Vt + V² / (30(a/32.2 ± G))

Example on level ground: V = 50 mph, t = 2.5 s, a = 11.2 ft/s². The reaction part is 1.47 × 50 × 2.5 = 183.8 ft. The braking part is 2,500 / (30 × 0.3478) = 2,500 / 10.43 = 239.6 ft. The total is about 423 ft, which lines up with the commonly tabulated 425 ft for 50 mph.

On a 4% downgrade the grade term is subtracted: 2,500 / (30 × 0.3078) = 270.7 ft of braking, for a total near 454 ft. The sign of G is the detail most often flipped under time pressure. The stopping sight distance and curve length unit ties this number to the vertical curve check that follows.

4. Crest vertical curve length

With a 3.5 ft eye height and a 2.0 ft object height, the crest curve length for S < L is L = AS² / 2,158, where A is the algebraic difference in grades in percent.

Example: a +3% grade meets a −4% grade, so A = 7. Using S = 425 ft, L = 7 × 180,625 / 2,158 = 585.9 ft. Because 585.9 ft is longer than 425 ft, the S < L assumption holds. If the result had come out shorter than S, the other form, L = 2S − 2,158/A, would be the one to use. Checking the assumption after solving is a quick habit that catches a lot of errors.

5. Traffic flow and peak hour factor

The basic identity is q = k × v: flow equals density times space-mean speed. In the Greenshields model, speed falls linearly with density, v = vf(1 − k/kj), and capacity occurs at half the jam density: q max = vf × kj / 4.

Example: free-flow speed 60 mph and jam density 200 veh/mi/lane give a capacity of 60 × 200 / 4 = 3,000 veh/h/lane, reached at 100 veh/mi and 30 mph.

The peak hour factor compares the full hour to its busiest 15 minutes: PHF = V / (4 × V15). With 15-minute counts of 410, 455, 520 and 470, the hourly volume is 1,855 and PHF = 1,855 / (4 × 520) = 0.892. The design flow rate is V / PHF = 2,080 veh/h. A PHF above 1.0 is always a sign of an inverted ratio. The traffic flow and capacity unit goes through level of service on the same footing.

6. Flexible pavement structural number

In the AASHTO flexible pavement method, each layer adds to the structural number:

SN = a1D1 + a2D2m2 + a3D3m3

Example: 4 in of asphalt (a1 = 0.44), 8 in of granular base (a2 = 0.14, m2 = 1.0) and 10 in of subbase (a3 = 0.11, m3 = 1.0). SN = 1.76 + 1.12 + 1.10 = 3.98. The drainage coefficients apply only to the unbound layers, never to the surface course.

A related planning calculation is the crash rate per million entering vehicles at an intersection: rate = crashes × 10⁶ / (365 × years × entering ADT). Twelve crashes in 3 years with 18,000 entering vehicles per day gives 12,000,000 / 19,710,000 = 0.61 crashes per million entering vehicles.

Turning formulas into exam speed

Reading the formulas is not the same as using them under a clock. The FE works out to a little under three minutes per item on average, and transportation problems often have one tempting distractor built from a sign error or a skipped term. Working a set of FE Civil transportation practice problems with the handbook open shows quickly which relationships you can find and apply in under three minutes and which ones still need review. A timed full-length run on AceFEExam then shows whether transportation is costing you more time than its share of the exam.
